**Productivity is how much money you get out for the time you put in**; I know this because I went from $0 at age 23 to a $100 million plus net worth at age 31 simply by better investing my time. I'm going to teach you how I do it so that hopefully you can do it, and I'm even going to show you my calendar at the end of the video and walk you through step by step how I get good returns on my time. Have you ever had someone reach out to you and say, "Hey, got a minute? Hey, got 10? Hey, let me introduce you to so-and-so, they're going to be in town—how does 10 a.m. work?". In a normal exchange like that, you might say, "Yeah, I've got 5 minutes," or "Sure, I can meet so-and-so for coffee". The problem is that for one specific type of worker, that **five-minute meeting blows their entire morning** because they can't do the development work they need—they can't edit the video, they can't write the book chapter—because it disrupts their workflow. On the other hand, there's another type of worker where for them it's actually very **optimal to take that meeting**.

The question is: which one are you, and at what times?. Also, a special shout-out to Paul Graham, who wrote a tiny blog on this 14 years ago. So, if time is what we invest and money is what we make from that investment, then the people who are the best investors of time are the ones who make the most money. It turns out there are **two specific types of entrepreneurs**, and you'll switch hats throughout your day or throughout your week. The first and most common investor of time that people are familiar with is the **manager schedule**, and these people have a very specific way of investing time and getting returns on it.

Managers divide their time into the **smallest chunk possible**; they often have 20-plus chunks per day from 15 minutes—sometimes 5 minutes—all the way to an hour or 90 minutes. For managers, an **empty time slot is a lost opportunity**; that's time that did not get a return on investment for them. For that reason, they treat time like currency, and the only real cost for them to fill it is just the cost of coordinating with somebody else's calendar to find a mutually empty slot so that they can work together. On paper, since work on meetings is a mutually filled slot, it makes both people more productive. When a manager interacts with another manager and they mutually fill a slot, both of them make more money. They talk to lots of different people and do many different kinds of tasks all day long; they collect data, report data to persuade, lead, train, encourage, and make other decisions one-on-one and sometimes in groups.

For them, they have a pretty clear beginning and end of the day; it begins when they start preparing for meetings—maybe 30 minutes or an hour beforehand—and ends at the end of their last meeting. If they prep for the next day, they could add that on top, but more or less, the duration of their day is directly proportional to the duration of the meetings they have. They basically work until their last calendar slot. The **main objective of the manager is to use up all their chunks in the day** so that they maximize their time; a fully booked day is a maximally invested manager.

The second type of time schedule management is the **maker**, which the vast majority of people do not know. This is where creators, entrepreneurs, and people who have to build big things get the highest returns on their time. This is the **Deep Work**—the stuff that moves the big ball forward. These are often tasks that are **not urgent but incredibly important**, and when you look back on your month, quarter, or year, the few things that actually move the business forward were these chunks of work.

The problem is most entrepreneurs do not have the time or system for maximizing that important work. If those are the footholds that allow you to push towards your goal, you want as many as possible. The difficulty is that it's never urgent. If you're a coder, developing software is the most valuable thing you can do; if you're an editor, editing the video is most important; if you're an author, writing the book is most important. All of those things are maker work where you create something that before this did not exist. Although this work is important, it's **incredibly expensive in terms of time**, and the problem is the vast majority of the world works on manager time.

For the maker, instead of many small slivers, you have only a **few large chunks**. If you put a meeting in or interrupt a four-hour block, you often can't get back to the work you were doing before. Also, if you know you have a block coming, you can't get into the flow because you know you'll have to get out of it. This is called the **Zeigarnik Effect**: we tend to remember open loops, and meetings are open loops because you have to set timers, start thinking about the meeting, and prepare for it, which eats up your brain power and saps your ability to make.

Unlike the manager, for whom coordination is the output, for the maker, the **things they build are the output** of their time. Instead of working *on* meetings, they work *off* meetings on projects that can last weeks, months, or quarters. Most makers have a set start time but **variable end times**—they work **"open to goal"**. If you've ever lost yourself in your work and wanted to keep riding that flow, that's what it feels like to make; having no cap allows you to produce more than you ever could.

Because you only have large time chunks, you usually have only two—sometimes three—per day: a morning block, a post-lunch block, and maybe a post-dinner block. If someone takes one meeting in the morning, they blew **10% of your total weekly output** if you work five days. The manager who puts that meeting in is becoming more productive, but they are **destroying your productivity**. This is how businesses stay small and entrepreneurs fail to grow, reliving the same 12 months over and over because they do not know how to invest their time. For the maker, an **empty time slot is the opportunity** to get the return. If you see a literally empty calendar, you feel filled with energy to "crush the day" and sink your teeth into a big project. That energy exists because an empty calendar represents a maximally productive day for a maker.

When I was writing my book, my days were just writing; people asked how I could do that while running a portfolio of companies, and I will show you how that makes sense on my calendar. Diametrically opposed calendars can both be maximally allocated to get the highest returns in terms of money. The big problem is when they need to interact. When a manager doesn't know how makers work, they **kill the productivity of their team**. Managers assume makers can work on demand; they look at a maker's empty calendar and think they are "barely even working" because they are using their own measuring stick. For a manager, a short meeting costs one of 20-plus units, but for a maker, it takes one of two daily slots. Furthermore, if you schedule a performance review for 2:00 p.m., the employee will worry about it all day beforehand and think about what they said all day after—that whole day is shot.

To make matters worse, if a maker falls behind, the manager's solution is to interrupt them more, creating a **vicious cycle** where managers prevent the work they are checking in on. By checking in, they disrupt the maker's work. In this scenario, both parties lose: the maker isn't making, and the manager is responsible for output they aren't getting. If you are on a team, share this language with your boss.

If a manager asks a maker for time, the maker either **offends the manager by saying no** or **destroys their morning by saying yes**. It's a terrible proposition. Often, the meeting yield is nothing because the person who asked for "five minutes" had no agenda and just stole your morning. This applies to networking too; "coffee at 10 a.m. to explore synergies" can light a whole day on fire. I know what I need to do to make more money, and I just need to do that. The highest predictor of my success is that I have been **ruthless with my time**. People know I am a "dick" with my time, but I maintain relationships because during that maker time, I keep moving things forward. I believe that as long as I continue to make valuable things, those connections and friend dinners will always be there.

In the beginning, it was hard because I didn't have accomplishments to show, but I believed that if I moved the ball forward every day, that would matter. Once I realized maker time was where all my progress happened, I optimized my life around it. Note that roles like CEO or COO require more manager tasks, but you must be clear that your job *is* the manager stuff, and maker time is used to get leverage on that. Leila and I have different schedules; she might have one maker day and four manager days, while I have four maker days and one manager day. She needs that maker day to get leverage on her other four days. You have to get out of either/or thinking and use the style best for the moment.

When these styles interact poorly, makers end up losing sleep and family time just to keep up with work without pissing anyone off. My **three-pronged approach** for solutions addresses managers, makers, and the organization.

**For Managers:**
1.  **Understand the cost:** You disrupt them twice—once while coordinating and once at the meeting. It costs them **10 times more** than it costs you. Ask yourself: "Can this wait until our set cadence?".
2.  **Value the "Maker's No":** See a declined meeting as a commitment to getting meaningful work done for the company. Don't hold back promotions because of it. If a meeting is truly critical, frame it that way and give them time to prep.
3.  **Ask for the "Ideal Day":** Ask your team what a maximally productive day looks like and try to accommodate it. For example, our editing team bunched all meetings together to keep the rest of their days free. Remember that not everyone needs to work on the same thing at the same time.

**For Makers:**
1.  **Communicate:** Good managers want to help you, so tell them how you work. You will still have to take meetings sometimes—it's a team effort.
2.  **Fight Fire with Fire:** If a meeting is put in the middle of your morning, switch that whole block to a **manager schedule** and knock out as many small meetings as possible. Don't identify as one or the other; let your calendar reflect the nature of the work.
3.  **Standard Meeting Times:** Have designated blocks (e.g., Monday and Thursday afternoons) for meetings. If a meeting isn't truly urgent, push it to those blocks.

I've gone through three periods of this. **V1** was doing maker time during **nights, weekends, and early mornings**. As a gym owner, I'd write ad copy at 5 a.m. before running the gym all day. Without that uninterrupted time, I never would have built the systems (training, nutrition, advertising) that allowed me to scale. **V2** is a **50/50 split**: first half maker, second half manager. Pro tip: **plan your day back-to-front** (starting from 5 p.m.) to keep the front of your day open. **V3** (my current version) is having **entire Maker Days** (2–4 per week) and stacking all meetings onto a single **Manager Day**.

**For the Organization:**
1.  **Communicate about communication:** Tell people when you'll be slow to respond. People only get upset at unmet expectations.
2.  **Actually work:** If people respect your schedule, you owe it to them not to waste that time; don't prove their suspicions that you're just "goofing off" right.
3.  **Mandated Quiet Time:** Consider times where entire teams can't message or meet. For our editors, Wednesdays are quiet days. This applies to makers like engineers, copywriters, and developers.
4.  **Trust and Output:** Remote work has removed transparency, but you shouldn't disrupt people with Slack just to see if they're working. Extend trust and **measure based on output**.
5.  **Audit Meetings:** Ask, "Do I need to do this again?" and "Is there a system or person that can do this instead?". Leila deletes meetings first and looks for ways to return time to people.

Entrepreneurship is a **relinquishing of control**. You cannot have both absolute control and absolute freedom. If you want freedom, you must extend trust. Every day you take the whole team to a meeting is like taking them to Chili's; a $500 meeting is a major expense. By respecting these styles, you will get **higher returns on human capital**, and your people will be happier with both higher quality and quantity of work. I made this for the makers whose work moves the world and to help managers understand how expensive a "got a minute" really is.
