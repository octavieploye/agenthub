## THE PRINCIPLE IN ONE SENTENCE

Productivity is how much money you get out for the time you put in — and the
return depends entirely on whether you're investing that time as a **Manager**
or a **Maker**.

---

## TWO MODES OF TIME INVESTMENT

Every entrepreneur, CEO, or team leader switches between two hats. The mistake
is not knowing which hat you're wearing — or letting someone else's hat destroy
your returns.

### Mode 1: Manager Schedule

**How it works:** Time is divided into the smallest chunks possible — 15 min to
90 min. 20+ slots per day.

| Attribute          | Manager Reality                                         |
|--------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| Empty slot         | Lost opportunity — time that earned no return            |
| Cost of a meeting  | Only the coordination cost to find a mutual slot         |
| Output             | Coordination: collecting data, reporting, persuading, leading, training, deciding |
| Day structure      | Clear start/end — proportional to meeting count          |
| Maximally invested | Fully booked calendar                                   |

**Key insight:** When a manager meets another manager and mutually fills a slot,
both become more productive. Meetings *are* the work.

### Mode 2: Maker Schedule

**How it works:** Time is divided into a few large blocks — typically 2-3 per day
(morning, post-lunch, post-dinner). Each block is 3-4+ hours of uninterrupted
Deep Work.

| Attribute          | Maker Reality                                           |
|--------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| Empty slot         | The opportunity — this is where the return happens       |
| Cost of a meeting  | Destroys 10x more value than it costs the requester      |
| Output             | Creation: code, writing, design, systems, products — things that didn't exist before |
| Day structure      | Fixed start, variable end — "open to goal"               |
| Maximally invested | Empty calendar                                          |

**Key insight:** A single 30-min meeting in a morning block doesn't cost 30 min —
it blows the entire block because the maker can't enter flow before or after.

---

## WHY THEY COLLIDE

- Managers see a maker's empty calendar and think "barely working" — they measure
  with their own ruler.
- A manager requesting "5 minutes" offloads one of 20+ daily slots but takes one
  of the maker's 2-3 daily blocks.
- If a maker falls behind, the manager's instinct is to check in more → creating a
  **vicious cycle** where the checking prevents the work being checked on.
- A 2 p.m. performance review costs the maker the entire day — worry before,
  rumination after (Zeigarnik Effect: open loops consume cognitive bandwidth).

**The maker's dilemma:** Say no → offend the manager. Say yes → destroy the morning.
Either way, the maker loses.

---

## THE ZEIGARNIK EFFECT

We remember unclosed loops. A scheduled meeting is an open loop:
- You set timers
- You start mentally preparing
- You can't enter deep flow knowing you'll be pulled out

This is why even a *future* meeting destroys *current* maker time.

---

## SOLUTIONS — THREE-PRONGED APPROACH

### For Managers (working with makers)

1. **Understand the 10x cost.** Your 15-min meeting costs them 10x that in lost
   deep work. Ask: "Can this wait until our set cadence?"
2. **Value the Maker's No.** A declined meeting is a commitment to meaningful
   output. Don't punish it. If truly critical, frame it that way and give prep time.
3. **Ask for the Ideal Day.** Ask each team member what a maximally productive day
   looks like, then accommodate it. Bunch meetings together to free contiguous blocks.

### For Makers (protecting their time)

1. **Communicate your mode.** Tell your manager/team how you work. Good managers
   want to help — but they can't if they don't know.
2. **Fight fire with fire.** If a meeting lands in your morning block, switch that
   entire block to manager mode — stack as many small meetings as possible. Don't
   identify as one or the other; let the calendar reflect the work.
3. **Standard meeting times.** Designate specific blocks (e.g., Monday and Thursday
   afternoons) for all meetings. Push non-urgent meetings to those windows.

### For the Organization

1. **Communicate about communication.** Tell people when you'll be slow to respond.
   People get upset at unmet expectations, not delayed replies.
2. **Actually work.** If people respect your schedule, don't waste the time you
   fought for. Deliver output.
3. **Mandated quiet time.** Team-wide no-meeting, no-Slack blocks (e.g., "Wednesday
   is quiet day for editors").
4. **Trust and output.** Don't disrupt people with pings just to verify they're
   working. Extend trust, measure by output.
5. **Audit meetings.** For every recurring meeting ask: "Do I need to do this again?"
   and "Is there a system or person that can handle this instead?"

## EVOLUTION — THREE VERSIONS

| Version | Pattern                        | When to use                                    |
|---------|--------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| V1      | Maker time = nights, weekends, early mornings | Starting out — no control over daytime schedule |
| V2      | 50/50 split — first half maker, second half manager | Growing — some calendar control. **Pro tip:** plan your day back-to-front (start from 5 p.m.) to keep mornings open |
| V3      | Entire Maker Days (2-4/week) + stacked Manager Days (1-2/week) | Scaled — full calendar sovereignty |

---

## HOW TO APPLY THIS

### For entrepreneurs / CEOs
- Identify your current version (V1/V2/V3) and work toward the next one.
- Audit your week: how many maker blocks actually stayed uninterrupted?
- Every meeting you accept during maker time costs 10% of weekly deep-work output
  (assuming 2 blocks/day × 5 days = 10 blocks total).

### The entrepreneurship paradox
Entrepreneurship is a **relinquishing of control**. You cannot have both absolute
control and absolute freedom. If you want freedom, you must extend trust.
