## WHAT SIMULATION MEANS HERE

Not probability bands. Not adoption curves. Not TAM percentages.

A simulation is a living, contextualized situation:
a specific person, in a specific country, at a specific cultural moment,
encountering the destructured business strategy offer for the first time,
with their full psychology, economic situation, and emotional state modeled —
and a grounded, honest answer to: do they convert, why or why not, and what would change the outcome?

The offer was engineered via Business Strategy Destructuring on validated market intelligence.
This phase answers whether the offer actually lands when it meets a real human being in a real country with a real set of beliefs about what spending money means.

## THE FOUNDATIONAL DIVIDE — SPENDING VS. INVESTMENT

Before building any scenario, establish the foundational divide that shapes everything.

**The EU/spend frame (most of Europe, North Africa, much of Asia):**
Spending money = making myself poorer.
A purchase is a cost to be justified, not an opportunity to be seized.
"Convince me this is worth more than I am giving up."
"More tools = more complexity, more dependency, more things to manage."
"Will this make my life harder before it makes it easier?"
Trust is earned slowly and lost fast. Peer validation precedes price discussion.
Even when budget exists, price sensitivity is cultural — not purely financial.
The burden of proof sits entirely on the seller.

**The US/invest frame:**
Spending money = opening opportunity.
A purchase is an investment. The question is not "can I afford it" but "what is my ROI?"
"Show me what this unlocks that I could not do before."
"What is my competitive edge if I have this and my competitor does not?"
"What is the cost of NOT buying this?"
Speed of decision is higher. Pain of inaction is as powerful as gain framing.
Price anchoring works: make visible what the buyer loses by waiting.

These are not stereotypes — they are structural orientations that shape the offer framing required per market.
An offer that closes in Austin will confuse in Lyon.
An offer that closes in Tokyo will fail in Lagos if the trust foundation is not built first.
The same product. The same features. Entirely different human truth.

### EUROPE

**France (FR)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a uniquely French layer: "I am not easily sold."
Primary trust signals: Intellectual credibility, craft, brand story with depth, endorsement from respected French figures or institutions — not influencers.
Primary objections: "Is this serious?" / "Is this American hype dressed up?" / "Why do I need this now?"
Price relationship: Price is secondary to legitimacy. A high price from a brand with credibility is acceptable. A low price with aggressive urgency is a red flag.
ROI language: "Gain in rigor", "clarity", "professional standing" — not "time savings" alone.
Social proof pattern: Peer referral within a professional circle. Case studies from French companies or European institutions. Third-party editorial coverage matters more than testimonials.
Risk tolerance: Low. Deliberation before commitment.
Buying speed: Slow. The decision cycle is longer than most Western markets.
Cultural objection: "This feels like it was made for Americans." If the product feels alien to their professional context, they disengage before evaluating it.
Emotional open door: Professional embarrassment or deadline pressure. When they fear being caught behind, they move.
Red flags: Aggressive sales language, countdown timers, "limited offer" framing, anything that feels like it is trying to rush their judgment.

**Germany (DE)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a German-specific variant: purchasing is an engineering decision.
Primary trust signals: Technical specification, reliability record, references from German companies, engineering credentials, certifications.
Primary objections: "Does this actually work without error?" / "What happens when something breaks?" / "Who else uses this in a professional context?"
Price relationship: Price is justified by specification, not by emotion or urgency. A premium price with full technical justification is accepted. Emotion-based pricing closes the conversation.
ROI language: "Reliability", "precision", "reduces failure rate", "documented performance improvement."
Social proof pattern: Case studies with measurable outcomes, references, technical reviews. Peer testimony from known German organizations.
Risk tolerance: Very low. The cultural cost of a visible public failure is high. They will not risk it.
Buying speed: Long decision cycle. But once decided — loyal and stable customers.
Cultural objection: "We already have a process that works." Disrupting a working process requires extraordinary evidence of improvement.
Emotional open door: When their current process visibly fails them — especially publicly. That is when they search.
Red flags: Vague claims, testimonials without specifics, marketing that overpromises, any sense that the product has not been tested at depth.

**Nordics — Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland (SE/NO/DK/FI)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a Nordic variant: "I buy what is functional, necessary, and ethically sound. Not more."
Primary trust signals: Community trust, word-of-mouth within trusted networks, sustainability credentials, transparent pricing with no hidden layers.
Primary objections: "Is this something I actually need?" / "Can I trust this company long-term?" / "Does this conflict with values I hold?"
Price relationship: Fair pricing is expected. Luxury pricing requires functional justification, not status justification. Scandinavian flat organizational psychology means the person buying is often also the person using — no distance between decision and experience.
ROI language: "Works reliably", "simplifies my work", "I trust it completely", "community-endorsed."
Social proof pattern: Within trusted professional or personal networks. Not influencers. Not authority figures. Peers they actually know.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Willing to try if the trust threshold is met. Fast to abandon if let down.
Buying speed: Deliberate but not slow. Once they trust, decision speed increases significantly.
Cultural objection: Status signaling in the pitch. If the product sounds like it is selling aspiration rather than function, they disengage. "Jante Law" residue — "who do you think you are to tell me what I need?"
Emotional open door: Efficiency gain in something they find genuinely frustrating. Practical relief is more powerful than transformation narrative.
Red flags: Hype language, grandiose claims, oversell, anything that sounds like an American commercial.

**Italy (IT)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with an Italian variant: "I buy from people I trust, not from products I evaluate."
Primary trust signals: The person behind the product. Relationship first — product second. Aesthetic and presentation quality signal seriousness.
Primary objections: "Who are you to me?" / "Does this look like a serious, considered thing?" / "Have my contacts used this?"
Price relationship: Price is negotiable and relational. A good relationship makes price flexible. A transactional relationship makes price the center of everything.
ROI language: "Quality", "elegance of solution", "trusted by people I respect."
Social proof pattern: Personal referral from someone in their network. Warm introduction is worth more than any case study.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Willing to try based on relationship trust. Very sensitive to being let down.
Buying speed: Slow initially. Fast once the relationship is established.
Cultural objection: Impersonal or purely transactional approach. "You are selling me, not serving me."
Emotional open door: When someone they respect tells them about something. The recommendation IS the open door.
Red flags: Corporate coldness, automated communication, no human presence in the sales process, poor aesthetics in product or communication.

**Spain (ES)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a Spanish variant: "I buy what my community validates."
Primary trust signals: Peer approval, relational warmth in the sales interaction, community endorsement.
Primary objections: "Is this necessary?" / "Will this make me look foolish if it does not work?" / "Who else in my circle uses it?"
Price relationship: Value for money matters, but not above relationship quality. An overpriced product from a trusted community member is more likely to sell than a fairly priced product from an unknown brand.
ROI language: "Works well for people like me", "trusted by my peers", "simplifies without complicating."
Social proof pattern: Community and peer network. Social media presence within their specific community matters. Endorsement from community figures, not generic celebrities.
Risk tolerance: Medium-low. Community approval reduces risk perception significantly.
Buying speed: Relationship-paced. Not slow if trust is built.
Cultural objection: Corporate impersonality. If the communication feels automated or generic, it is rejected.
Emotional open door: Seeing someone in their community succeed with it. FOMO within the community is a real driver.
Red flags: Aggressive urgency, hard sell, anything that feels pressuring or manipulative.

**Netherlands (NL)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a Dutch variant: "Show me exactly what I get, at a fair price, with no nonsense."
Primary trust signals: Transparency, specificity, honest acknowledgment of limitations, clear pricing with no hidden layers.
Primary objections: "Is the price fair?" / "Is this overcomplicated?" / "Are they being straight with me?"
Price relationship: Price/value ratio is calculated directly and rationally. If it is fair — they pay. If it is inflated — they walk, publicly.
ROI language: "Does what it says", "worth exactly what it costs", "no surprises."
Social proof pattern: Independent reviews, comparisons, technical documentation. Peer referral matters but data-backed claims also carry weight.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Will try if the case is clear. Will not forgive hidden complexity after purchase.
Buying speed: Fast if the case is made clearly. Decisive.
Cultural objection: Oversell. If you claim more than you can deliver, they will catch it and they will tell others. Calvinist cultural residue: spending on unnecessary things requires justification, and the seller who inflates the need is distrusted.
Emotional open door: Clear, honest demonstration of value — no theater, just the thing working.
Red flags: Vague benefits, inflated testimonials, urgency manufactured to bypass evaluation, anything that does not survive direct questioning.

**Eastern Europe — Poland, Czech Republic, Hungary, Romania (PL/CZ/HU/RO)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with a post-Soviet pragmatism: "I have been oversold before. Prove it."
Primary trust signals: Concrete results, references, working demo, longevity of the company. Skepticism toward new entrants.
Primary objections: "Is this actually stable?" / "Will this company exist in a year?" / "Is the price the real price or will it increase?"
Price relationship: Price sensitivity is real and cultural. A good product at a fair price wins. Pricing changes after purchase are a trust destruction event.
ROI language: "Reliable", "stable", "proven", "saves real money."
Social proof pattern: References from regional companies, local user communities, technical forums. Western marketing language is discounted.
Risk tolerance: Low. History of instability creates conservatism in purchasing decisions.
Buying speed: Deliberate. Especially for business purchasing. Consumer decisions can be faster.
Cultural objection: Overpromising. The post-Soviet experience created strong pattern-matching for "things that sound too good." Anything that sounds too smooth is immediately suspect.
Emotional open door: Seeing peers succeed with it — especially if those peers are in similar economic and structural situations. "If it works for a company like mine in Warsaw, it works."
Red flags: Aggressive US-style sales tactics, urgency framing, lack of transparency about limitations, pricing that changes after commitment.

### MIDDLE EAST

**Gulf States — UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar (AE/SA/QA)**
Core buying frame: Invest — but filtered through status, face, and relationship. "The right people buy the right things from the right people."
Primary trust signals: Who introduced this. The brand's standing in the region. Whether using this product elevates or diminishes status. Hospitality and relationship quality of the seller.
Primary objections: "Is this from a brand that belongs in my world?" / "Who do I know that uses this?" / "Is this relationship worth building?"
Price relationship: Price is less primary than positioning for executive-level buying. For operational purchasing, price becomes more relevant. Premium pricing with a status signal works. Bargain pricing signals low standing.
ROI language: "Trusted by leaders in the region", "elevates the capability of the organization", "reflects a commitment to excellence."
Social proof pattern: Relationships and personal referral through social circles. Who you know who uses it matters more than what the product does.
Risk tolerance: Low for visible decisions (face protection). High for private decisions.
Buying speed: Slow to build relationship; fast once trust is established and status is confirmed.
Cultural objection: Transactional coldness. Skipping the relationship to get to the sale is an insult to the cultural convention of hospitality and trust-building.
Emotional open door: Recognition of standing. When the seller demonstrates genuine respect for the buyer's position and context, the door opens.
Red flags: Rushing the relationship, generic communication that ignores regional specifics, Islamic finance conflicts if applicable, anything that makes the buyer look like they were "sold to" rather than that they "chose."

### NORTH AFRICA

**Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Egypt (MA/DZ/TN/EG)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — with French cultural overlay in Maghreb, Arabic business culture layer underneath. "Trust the person, negotiate the price."
Primary trust signals: Personal relationship, community referral, local presence or local partner, cultural fluency in the seller's communication.
Primary objections: "Do I know this person or someone who does?" / "Is the price the real starting price?" / "Will this work in my context — not a Western context?"
Price relationship: Price negotiation is cultural, not adversarial. A fixed price without room for discussion can feel like a lack of respect. The negotiation IS part of the trust-building.
ROI language: Context-dependent: business buyers respond to efficiency and reliability; younger tech buyers respond to innovation and competitive edge.
Social proof pattern: Community and family network referrals. Local champions. Testimonials from people in the same cultural context — not European or American references.
Risk tolerance: Low for formal organizational buying. Higher for younger, tech-savvy segments.
Buying speed: Relationship-paced. Once trust is established, speed increases. Without relationship, the cycle is long.
Cultural objection: Absence of local relevance. If the product or communication feels like it was made somewhere else for someone else and adapted poorly, it is rejected.
Emotional open door: Demonstrated respect for the culture and genuine local presence — not just translated content.
Red flags: Price that cannot be discussed, communication that feels generic or copied from Western markets, no local references or case studies.

### AFRICA

**Central and West Africa — Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya (NG/GH/KE)**
Core buying frame: Hybrid. Ubuntu-influenced community psychology — "I am because we are." Purchase decisions ripple outward through networks.
Primary trust signals: Community endorsement, local champion, word-of-mouth within trusted circles, mobile-accessible proof of concept.
Primary objections: "Will this work on the infrastructure I have?" / "Who in my network can vouch for this?" / "Is this built for people like me or adapted for me as an afterthought?"
Price relationship: Value consciousness is high, but not purely price-driven. A product that works and is vouched for by the community commands willingness to pay. Mobile-first payment patterns matter for friction.
ROI language: "Practical", "solves the real problem", "works here", "trusted by people like me."
Social proof pattern: Oral and network culture. Personal referral is far more powerful than written review. WhatsApp communities, local professional networks, and respected community figures are primary channels.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Willing to try if the community has tested it. High skepticism toward untested foreign brands.
Buying speed: Community validation can be fast — once a trusted person endorses, adoption can cascade through a network quickly.
Cultural objection: Colonial-feeling product design or communication — something that treats the African buyer as a secondary market. "This was made for them, not for us."
Emotional open door: Genuine local relevance. A product that solves a problem specific to their operating environment — infrastructure, mobile access, local economic conditions — and demonstrates that it was built understanding those conditions.
Red flags: Product that requires infrastructure it cannot assume, pricing in currencies without local anchoring, communication that ignores local context entirely.

**South Africa (ZA)**
Core buying frame: Hybrid between UK/EU spend caution and US-adjacent invest frame, depending on demographic and economic tier.
Primary trust signals: Track record, peer referral within professional networks, brand recognition, local presence.
Primary objections: "Is this tested in our market?" / "What support exists locally?" / "Will this scale to our complexity?"
Price relationship: Value for money, but more sophisticated than price alone. Business buyers evaluate TCO (total cost of ownership). Consumer buyers are price-sensitive in lower income tiers.
ROI language: "Proven locally", "reliable", "scales with our context."
Social proof pattern: Professional networks, LinkedIn in business contexts, word-of-mouth in community contexts.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Urban professional class more willing to adopt new tools. Township and rural markets are relationship-first.
Buying speed: Varies by sector. Government and large enterprise: slow. SME and startup: faster.
Cultural objection: Ignoring the complexity of the South African market. Treating ZA as one homogeneous market is immediately evident and off-putting to sophisticated buyers.
Emotional open door: Local relevance with real case studies from South African organizations operating in similar conditions.
Red flags: One-size-fits-all messaging, no local support path, pricing that ignores local purchasing power parity.

### ASIA

**Japan (JP)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — elevated to an extreme: "The decision must be correct. We do not rush correct decisions."
Primary trust signals: Precision, quality of documentation, after-sales service reputation, longevity of the company, relationships built over time.
Primary objections: "Is this error-free?" / "What happens if something goes wrong, and who is responsible?" / "Has this been adopted by respected Japanese organizations?"
Price relationship: Premium price for premium quality is understood and accepted. Low price signals low quality. The concern is not price — it is reliability and after-service.
ROI language: "Flawless performance", "dependable support", "adopted by [respected Japanese institution]."
Social proof pattern: References from respected Japanese organizations, technical documentation, long-term case studies. Seniority in the referring organization matters.
Risk tolerance: Very low. The cultural cost of recommending something that fails is significant. Group decision-making means no individual wants to be the person who championed a failure.
Buying speed: Very slow. Nemawashi (consensus-building) must happen across multiple stakeholders before any decision. But once decided — committed and stable.
Cultural objection: Skipping the process. Trying to accelerate the Japanese buying cycle is read as disrespect and lack of seriousness.
Emotional open door: Visible adoption by a respected Japanese peer organization. If Sony or Toyota uses it, the risk of adoption is socialized.
Red flags: Errors or bugs in any demo or trial, imprecise documentation, lack of Japanese language support, communication that ignores the hierarchy of the buying organization.

**South Korea (KR)**
Core buying frame: Invest — but through a strong brand-status filter. "The right brand at the right moment, adopted before the competition."
Primary trust signals: Brand standing, innovation credentials, adoption by major Korean companies, K-culture aesthetic alignment.
Primary objections: "Is this brand credible enough for my organization?" / "Are my competitors already using this?" / "Does this reflect innovation or is it behind?"
Price relationship: Price is secondary to brand and status signal. Premium pricing from a credible brand is fine. Budget pricing is suspicious.
ROI language: "Competitive edge", "adopted by leading Korean companies", "reflects innovation leadership."
Social proof pattern: Industry adoption by recognized Korean organizations. Fast-moving networks. If it is trending among peers, adoption accelerates rapidly.
Risk tolerance: Medium-high. Korea is an early adopter culture. Being second is not the goal.
Buying speed: Fast once organizational champions are convinced. Organizational hierarchy still matters — senior buy-in accelerates everything.
Cultural objection: The brand is unknown or the product aesthetic does not meet Korean standards of quality presentation. South Korea has extremely high design and quality standards.
Emotional open door: Competitive threat. "Your competitor is already using this" is one of the most effective triggers.
Red flags: Poor design quality, brand that cannot demonstrate credibility in the Korean market, anything that signals being behind the innovation curve.

**China (CN)**
Core buying frame: Complex — varies enormously by buyer tier. State/enterprise: relationship and policy-aligned. Private business: ROI + guanxi. Consumer: status in middle/upper tier, value in mass market.
Primary trust signals: Guanxi (relationships and connections). Who introduced this. Whether using it aligns with current policy and regulatory direction. Local presence and Chinese language depth.
Primary objections: "Is this compliant with local regulations?" / "Who is the relationship behind this?" / "Is there a Chinese alternative and why is this better?"
Price relationship: Negotiation is expected at enterprise level. Consumer market is extremely price-competitive in mass tier. Premium tier is status-driven and less price-sensitive.
ROI language: Varies by tier: efficiency and scale for enterprise; status and innovation for premium consumer.
Social proof pattern: Guanxi-network referral. WeChat ecosystem engagement. KOL (Key Opinion Leader) endorsement for consumer markets. Government-adjacent references for enterprise.
Risk tolerance: Variable. Private entrepreneurs: high. State-adjacent organizations: very low (compliance risk).
Buying speed: Enterprise: slow (political and relationship processes). Consumer: can be extremely fast once a trend ignites.
Cultural objection: Data sovereignty and privacy. Any product that cannot demonstrate data localization faces an immediate organizational compliance objection. Also: the product must be accessible within Chinese internet infrastructure.
Emotional open door: Recognition of standing and face. The framing "chosen by leading Chinese organizations" carries more weight than global adoption statistics.
Red flags: Non-compliance with Chinese data law, inaccessibility on Chinese infrastructure, communication that ignores the Chinese context entirely or treats China as a secondary market.

**Southeast Asia — Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia (SG/TH/VN/ID/MY)**
Core buying frame: Hybrid — Singapore is closest to UK/US invest frame; Thailand/Vietnam/Indonesia are relationship-first with EU/spend caution.
Primary trust signals: Local language presence, peer network referral, demonstrated understanding of local market conditions, mobile-first accessibility.
Primary objections: "Is this relevant to my market?" / "Who do I know that uses this?" / "Will this work on mobile and local infrastructure?"
Price relationship: Price sensitivity is real across most of the region, with Singapore as an exception. Value for money must be demonstrated clearly.
ROI language: "Practical", "works here", "trusted by local businesses", "mobile-ready."
Social proof pattern: Social media communities (LINE in Thailand, WhatsApp across the region), peer networks, local business communities.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Urban professional and startup segments are faster adopters. Traditional business is more conservative.
Buying speed: Varies: Singapore is fast and data-driven. Rest of the region is relationship-paced.
Cultural objection: Ignoring the linguistic and cultural diversity of the region. Treating "Southeast Asia" as one market is immediately visible and off-putting. Thai buyers, Vietnamese buyers, and Indonesian buyers are operating in different cultural worlds.
Emotional open door: Local success story. A company they have heard of in their country using this and succeeding is worth more than global statistics.
Red flags: English-only interface for non-Singapore markets, pricing in USD without local context, no local support or partnership.

**India (IN)**
Core buying frame: EU/spend — but with enormous internal diversity. North, South, East, West India are meaningfully different buyer cultures. Startup/tech segment is invest-frame.
Primary trust signals: Community network referral, track record in Indian market, local case studies, price that respects local purchasing power.
Primary objections: "Is the price fair for the Indian market?" / "Who in my network uses this?" / "Will this work with Indian infrastructure and workflows?" / "Can I negotiate?"
Price relationship: Price negotiation is culturally expected in many segments. "Jugaad" mindset (frugal innovation) means buyers will find or build alternatives if price is not aligned with perceived value. Startup segment increasingly willing to pay Western prices for the right product.
ROI language: "Saves cost", "proven track record in India", "scales with growth", "replaces X manual processes."
Social proof pattern: Word-of-mouth within professional communities, LinkedIn in tech/startup circles, local press for enterprise, family/community networks for SME.
Risk tolerance: Medium. Startup segment: high. Enterprise and SME: lower, requiring more proof.
Buying speed: Highly variable. Startup: fast. Enterprise: long cycle. Consumer: impulse-sensitive at right price point.
Cultural objection: Pricing without India-specific anchoring. A product priced for the US market without an India tier is immediately rejected by most of the market regardless of its value.
Emotional open door: Peer success within their specific professional community or city ecosystem. "Everyone in the Bangalore startup scene is using this" carries enormous weight within that community.
Red flags: No India-specific pricing, no local support, communication that treats India as a homogeneous market, anything that signals the company does not understand local operating conditions.

### AUSTRALIA

**Australia (AU)**
Core buying frame: Closer to US invest frame, but filtered through British understatement and "tall poppy syndrome."
Primary trust signals: Peer recommendation ("mate told me about it"), honest communication about limitations, product that works without oversell.
Primary objections: "Is this actually as good as they say?" / "Is this relevant to the Australian market?" / "Am I being oversold?"
Price relationship: Value for money matters. Not extreme price sensitivity, but Australians are attuned to when they are being charged a premium without justification. Will pay for quality but resist paying for hype.
ROI language: "Does what it says", "saves real time", "proven", "mate recommended."
Social proof pattern: Peer referral, community forums, honest reviews. Australians are early adopters in tech cities (Sydney, Melbourne) but require genuine proof of value before recommending to others.
Risk tolerance: Medium-high. Willing to try new things but will walk away and tell others if let down.
Buying speed: Faster than most European markets. Direct communication is valued.
Cultural objection: Tall poppy syndrome — if the product or seller comes across as arrogant, claims to be extraordinary, or oversells itself, Australian buyers actively distrust and disengage. "Who do you think you are?"
Emotional open door: A direct, honest problem statement that matches their exact frustration. No theater. Just: "You have this problem. Here is how this solves it."
Red flags: American-style hype, grandiose claims, urgency manufactured through artificial scarcity, anything that sounds like a pitch rather than a conversation.

## BUILDING THE SCENARIO SET

Minimum 5 scenarios. Maximum 10.
Every scenario must be distinct — a different combination of axes that tests a genuinely different market reality.

**The scenario axes:**

1. **Persona** — which persona from Phase 1 (use the exact persona card)
2. **Country/Culture** — which geo track, using the profile above
3. **Pricing tier** — which offer tier or ladder step from the destructured business strategy
4. **Market trend context** — what macro trend is active in this scenario
5. **Recent event** — something that happened in the world that shifts relevance or urgency RIGHT NOW
6. **Buyer's economic situation** — comfortable / constrained / growth mode / survival mode
7. **Emotional trigger** — what specific moment or frustration brought this person to the offer TODAY

Each scenario must vary on at least 3 axes from every other scenario.
Do not run 5 scenarios on the same persona with different countries only — that is a geo split, not a scenario set.
Include at least 1 constrained-budget scenario.
Include at least 1 scenario where EU-spend frame is active and 1 where US-invest frame is active.
Include at least 1 scenario from a non-Western market.

## CROSS-SCENARIO SYNTHESIS

After all scenario cards are complete:

**1. Offer stress-test map**
For each offer tier from the destructured business strategy: across which scenarios does it perform? Across which does it fail? What is the common failure mode across failing scenarios?

**2. Cultural adaptation matrix**
Which elements of the offer MUST change by geography to convert? Which are universal?
Name specific framing changes — not "adapt the messaging" but "replace 'saves you time' with 'eliminates the cost of doing it twice'" for [country].

**3. The highest-leverage scenario**
Which single scenario, if solved, unlocks the most market? Why?

**4. The scenario that revealed the critical blind spot**
Which scenario revealed something the research phases did not surface? What is the implication?

**5. Sequencing recommendation**
Based on the simulation: which geography + persona + offer tier combination has the highest P(convert) right now, with the resources available? Name it. This is the launch sequence candidate — not a recommendation, a simulation conclusion. Human confirms.
